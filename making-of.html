From one sentence to eight singing engines

The brief

“Lets make a real 3D interactive car engine website. I want you to research and comeback with a fun and creative idea.”

That was the whole brief. I wanted to see how far a clear direction and good judgement calls could take an AI build.

Research changed the idea

Before writing any code, Claude looked at what already existed. The obvious idea, a 3D engine you can rotate, take apart and rev, has been done well several times: Bartosz Ciechanowski's engine explainer, CrankLab, and AngeTheGreat's famously realistic engine simulator. Building another one would have been a copy.

The research turned up a better angle. An engine's pitch is its firing frequency, RPM × cylinders ÷ 120, so a V8 at 3,300 rpm hums the note A3 at exactly 220 Hz. In 2003 an F1 team programmed a V10 to play When the Saints Go Marching In this way. Nobody seemed to have made that playable in a browser, so that became the concept. Every engine is an instrument.

Who decided what

I decided

  • The brief, and which of three proposed concepts to build
  • The look: a dark photo studio with polished metal and a glass cutaway
  • Where it lives, and how it is credited
  • To add a Mazda rotary and the Lexus 4GR-FSE V6, and which rotary: the RX-8's Renesis
  • To turn it into this portfolio piece

Claude Code did

  • Researched existing engine sites and the physics, and found the gap
  • Proposed three concepts with honest trade-offs and limits
  • Wrote the physics, the sound synthesis, the 3D models and the interface
  • Wrote 146 tests, checked every screen in a browser at desktop and phone sizes
  • Reported what it could not check, such as how the sound feels to a human ear

How it works

  • Real crankshafts. Each engine's firing order is checked against its crank geometry. The V8 comes out as a true crossplane, and the 60° V6 needs split crank pins offset by 60° to fire evenly, which the 3D crank shows.
  • Real shaking. The engines rock on their mounts according to the forces their pistons actually leave unbalanced. The inline-4 buzzes; the inline-6 and V12 sit still.
  • A real rotary. The Wankel's rotor shape is computed as the largest shape that fits inside its housing at every shaft angle. A test checks it never touches the housing, and the chamber volume comes out at Mazda's 654 cc.
  • Sound from physics. Nothing is recorded. Each cylinder's exhaust pulse is synthesised at the moment its valve opens, then shaped by the pipes, so the V8's burble and the boxer's rumble come out of the firing order itself.

How I know it's right

The core claim is that each engine sounds at its firing frequency, so every engine is tested for exactly that: the test renders the sound, detects its pitch and requires it to land within 1% of the formula. The other tests cover the crankshafts, balance, the rotary's geometry, rev limiters, note holding and the game's scoring.

Checking found real problems, and each one was fixed before shipping:

  • The "ECU" that holds a note took almost two seconds to settle because its controller wound up. A test caught it; it now settles in under 0.6 seconds.
  • The first render floated the engine high above its stand, because an invisible glow effect counted toward its size. A screenshot review caught it.
  • The boxer's unequal exhaust headers were first set too long and smeared its pitch. They were cut to realistic lengths and checked with a spectrum test.
  • The rotary's rotors were hidden behind the front pulley. That engine now gets its own camera angle and a smaller pulley.
  • A slow test browser showed the sound falling behind when frames drop. The engine now runs on real time, so slow devices still sound right.

What it doesn't do

  • The sound is synthesised. Its pitch and rhythm follow the physics, but it will not fool you into thinking it is a recording.
  • The camera runs in slow motion by default so you can follow the crank. The sound always plays in real time.
  • Some engine figures are estimates where no published source exists, such as the V6's rod length and the rotary's port timing.
